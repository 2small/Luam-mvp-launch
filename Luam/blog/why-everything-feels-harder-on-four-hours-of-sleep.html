Why everything feels harder on four hours of sleep

It is 3 p.m. You have slept four hours. Lunch was tea and a mandazi. A colleague sends a two-line email and your chest tightens as if it were an insult.

You snap back. Then you wonder what is wrong with you.

Maybe nothing is wrong with you. Maybe your brain is running on empty, and an under-rested brain is quicker to read everything as a threat.

What your brain is doing

Two parts matter here. The amygdala is your alarm: it flags anything that might be a threat. The prefrontal cortex is your brakes: it lets you think before you react.

In 2007, researchers at UC Berkeley scanned the brains of 26 healthy young adults. After 35 hours without sleep, their amygdala was over 60% more reactive to upsetting images than after a normal night. The link between the alarm and the thinking part of the brain also appeared to weaken.

That was an extreme case, and a four-hour night is milder. But it shows the direction: with too little sleep, the alarm gets louder and the brakes appear to get weaker.

The other half of the story: movement

The World Health Organization recommends adults get at least 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week. That is about 21 to 43 minutes a day. It also says all physical activity counts, including walking, cycling, and household tasks, and that being active can reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety.

You do not need a gym. You need to move a little, most days.

Try it now: two minutes

  1. Drink a glass of water.
  2. Stand up. Reach both arms overhead and roll your shoulders back, 30 seconds.
  3. Walk for two minutes. To the gate, up the stairs, once around the compound.

Do not judge the result. You are turning the engine over, not finishing the journey.

Trying harder at midnight rarely helps. Changing what happens an hour earlier often does.

When it is more than this

If you have struggled to sleep for weeks, wake exhausted every day, or your body feels heavy alongside low mood, speak to a clinician. Sleep problems are common and treatable.

If you are in danger or thinking of ending your life, call 999 or 112 now, or open the Emergency page.
