One word to turn down the kelele

You are in a meeting. A message lands on your phone: three words, no greeting. Your jaw tightens. Your heart picks up speed. Your mind fills with kelele, a noise of what-ifs and replies you would never send.

You keep nodding at the slide. Inside, you are somewhere else.

Most of us try to push through. There is a quieter move, and it takes about 30 seconds.

What your brain is doing

In 2007, a UCLA team led by Matthew Lieberman scanned 30 adults as they looked at photos of faces showing emotions. For each face, they either picked an emotion word, such as angry or fearful, or picked a first name, such as Harry or Sally.

Only the emotion words calmed the brain's alarm. The amygdala responded less, and a thinking area at the front of the brain (the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex) became more active. In plain words: putting a feeling into a word seems to give the thinking part of your brain a handle on the alarm part.

That was a lab task, not therapy. But it is a simple idea you can test on yourself in half a minute.

The breath that goes with it

In 2023, a Stanford team ran a trial with 108 adults who practised for five minutes a day for 28 days. The group doing “cyclic sighing” (two breaths in through the nose, then one long breath out) improved in positive mood more than the group doing mindfulness meditation.

It was a small, early study, so treat it as a promising signal. The key idea is a long breath out. We use a simpler version below, a close cousin of what was tested rather than a proven copy.

Try it now: two minutes

  1. Notice. Where does it sit? Jaw, chest, stomach, shoulders. Ten seconds.
  2. Name. Choose one word: tense, worried, angry, sad, ashamed, tired, lonely, overwhelmed. Say it quietly: “I notice I feel ___.”
  3. Breathe. In for 4, out for 6. Five times.
  4. Adjust. Ask: what is one small thing I can change? Step outside, drop one task, drink water.

If the word you pick does not feel right, try another. Finding the closest word is part of the work.

Make it stick

Attach it to something you already do:

If–then planIf I sit down at my desk, then I name one feeling before I open my laptop.

When it is more than this

If strong feelings are frequent, last for weeks, come with panic, or get in the way of work and relationships, a licensed psychologist can help. This is the kind of skill therapy teaches, and you do not need to wait for a crisis.

If you are in danger or thinking of ending your life, call 999 or 112 now, or open the Emergency page.

Neno moja la kupunguza kelele

Uko kwenye mkutano. Ujumbe unaingia kwenye simu: maneno matatu, hakuna salamu. Taya lako linakaza. Moyo wako unaenda kasi. Kichwani kuna kelele ya “vipi kama”.

Utafiti wa UCLA ulionyesha kuwa kutaja hisia kwa neno moja kulipunguza mwitikio wa sehemu ya ubongo inayotoa kengele ya hatari.

Fanya sasa (dakika mbili): 1) Tambua hisia iko wapi mwilini. 2) Itaje kwa neno moja: nimechoka, nina wasiwasi, nina hasira, nina huzuni, nina aibu, nimezidiwa. 3) Vuta pumzi kwa hesabu ya 4, toa kwa hesabu ya 6, mara tano.

Ifanye kuwa mazoea: Nikikaa kwenye meza yangu, basi nitataja hisia moja kabla ya kufungua kompyuta.

Makala haya ni kwa ustawi wa jumla na si ushauri wa kitabibu wala utambuzi. Ukiwa hatarini, piga 999 au 112, au fungua ukurasa wa Dharura.
